\section[LEO satellite HO and FLSO schemes]{LEO satellite HO and FLSO schemes}
\quad In an NTN environment, the continuity of the service link between the UE and the satellite is ensured through HO. HO is the procedure in Fig. \ref{figure_bho_procedure} by which a UE changes the serving cell. Generally, this network-controlled procedure consists of three main phases: preparation, execution, and completion. Initially, the source gNB configures the UE to perform measurements on neighboring cells, and the UE sends a MR back to the network. Based on this report and other RRM information, the source gNB decides to initiate a HO and sends a HO REQUEST to a target gNB. The target gNB performs admission control, prepares resources, and responds with a HO REQUEST ACK. The source gNB then commands the UE to switch to the target cell by sending an RCR message. Upon receiving this, the UE synchronizes with the target cell and sends an RCRC message to finalize the process. Subsequently, the target gNB requests the core network to switch the data path and triggers the release of the UE's resources at the source gNB. However, applying this procedure in NTNs presents unique challenges. Unlike in terrestrial networks, the difference in received signal strength between satellites is often small, making traditional measurement-based triggers less reliable. Consequently, distance- or time-based HO criteria are also employed to manage the predictable mobility of satellites. In addition to the service link, the continuity of the feeder link connecting the satellite to the ground GW is equally important. The presence or absence of a gNB onboard the satellite determines whether a feeder link switchover impacts the service link. For a transparent satellite without an onboard gNB, a feeder link switchover changes the serving GW on the ground, which in turn changes the serving gNB, resulting in a necessary HO procedure for the UE. In contrast, for a regenerative satellite equipped with an onboard gNB, a feeder link switchover can be handled at the transport layer without changing the serving gNB, making the switch transparent to the UE and avoiding an HO.

\section[Reinforcement learning-based Beam hopping]{Reinforcement learning-based Beam hopping}
\quad BH has been extensively studied as a key resource-allocation technique to cope with spatially and temporally heterogeneous ground traffic. In LEO satellite systems, multi-satellite cooperation becomes a central challenge due to overlapping coverage and rapidly varying link conditions.

Lin \textit{et al.}~\cite{beamhopping_loadbalance} proposed a framework that decomposes the multi-satellite BH problem into two core components: load balancing and interference avoidance. Exploiting the multiple-coverage property of LEO constellations, the framework first distributes hotspot traffic across several satellites load balancing. Each satellite then independently designs a BH pattern for its assigned cells to satisfy per-cell demand while maintaining spatial isolation among simultaneously illuminated beams. Finally, the sequence of per-satellite BH patterns is jointly scheduled to mitigate inter-satellite interference avoidance. While this decomposition provides a clear problem structure, the approach in~\cite{beamhopping_loadbalance} relies on predefined heuristics to solve the subproblems sequentially, limiting adaptability to non-stationary traffic and unpredictable network dynamics.

Complementary to the above line, Xie \textit{et al.} propose a DRL approach for multi-LEO BH with joint beam scheduling and power allocation~\cite{beamhopping_drl}. Their method formulates a hybrid action space—discrete beam-illumination patterns and continuous per-beam power—and employs PPO with two policy heads sharing a common feature extractor. The objective jointly optimizes long-term throughput and long-term cumulative average delay under full-frequency reuse, explicitly accounting for both intra- and inter-satellite interference in the SINR model. Simulation results show notable gains over heuristic baselines (e.g., up to 8.9\% higher throughput and up to 69.2\% lower LTCAD), highlighting the potential of DRL for traffic-driven BH in time-varying environments. Nonetheless, the learning agent is effectively centralized and assumes access to global network state (e.g., via NOCC), and the model does not explicitly consider GW selection or feeder-link switchover effects on service continuity.

To address these limitations, this paper adopts a multi-agent reinforcement learning MARL formulation. We model each satellite as an autonomous agent that observes local network states (e.g., per-cell demand, channel/interference conditions, and GW status) and coordinates with other agents to learn a cooperative BH policy. The learned policy simultaneously optimizes interference avoidance and gateway load balancing, thereby improving service SINR and alleviating traffic bottlenecks. In contrast to the heuristic decomposition in~\cite{beamhopping_loadbalance} and the centralized DRL paradigm in~\cite{beamhopping_drl}, our MARL framework natively supports distributed decision-making under partial observability and explicitly couples BH with GW selection and feeder-link switchover, which are critical for end-to-end performance in practical NTN deployments.

% \quad Basic HO (BHO) is the traditional HO method where the serving cell initiates and controls the HO process \cite{enric_5g_new_radio}. First, when the HO conditions are satisfied for a certain period, known as the time-to-trigger (TTT), the UE sends a measurement report (MR) to the serving cell. Upon receiving the MR, the serving cell evaluates the measurement results and selects the most suitable target cell for the UE, making the HO decision. The serving cell then sends a HO request message to the target cell over the Xn interface to check if HO is possible. After receiving the HO Request acknowledgment (ACK) message from the target cell, the serving cell sends the HO command to the UE. Upon receiving the HO command, the UE immediately disconnects from the serving cell and attempts random access (RA) with the selected target cell. The entire procedure is detailed in Fig. \ref{figure_bho_procedure}.

This can lead to the selection of suboptimal target cells, as the MR used to make HO decisions are based on the signal conditions from previous moments. This issue is made worse by the dynamic environment of LEO satellite cells, where the rapid movement of satellites makes precise timing for HOs important to prevent UHOs or RLF.

Furthermore, due to the effects of signaling fading, there is a risk of selecting an incorrect target cell, leading to UHO or RLF. Therefore, employing the traditional HO method in NTN environments may degrade network performance.

% \quad To address the issues mentioned earlier, NTN adopts the CHO mechanism, initially proposed in NR \cite{rel_38300}. CHO divides the HO process into two different phases: the preparation and the execution phase. Similar to the conventional method, when the HO condition is met, the UE sends an MR to the serving cell. However, in CHO, instead of selecting a single target cell, the serving cell prepares multiple candidate target cells based on the MR and initiates the HO request message procedure. After receiving the HO Request ACK message from these candidate cells, the serving cell sends the HO command to the UE. At this point, rather than immediately executing the HO, the UE evaluates the prepared candidate cells through an execution event and selects the optimal target cell for the HO. This approach enhances flexibility in target cell selection, especially in LEO satellite environments, helping to reduce UHO and RLF. The complete procedure of CHO is illustrated in Fig. \ref{figure_cho_procedure}.
